Terminology

Bond prices and yields move inversely, so a yield that rises is a price that falls: a "selloff" and a "rise in yields" describe the same event. Every figure on this page is a yield, never a price.

↑Yields up · prices down

The holder loses; the buyer is paid more to lend.

sold off, selloff
Buyers stepped back — yields rose, prices fell.
rose, higher in yield
The plain form. What rose is the yield, so the price fell.
backed up
Yields moved higher quickly, usually against the holder.
cheapened
The bond got cheaper: lower price, higher yield.
bear steepening
Yields rose across the curve, the long end most.
bear flattening
Yields rose across the curve, the front end most.
hawkish, a hawk
Leaning towards tighter policy. A hawkish surprise lifts yields, the front end most — a bear flattening.
concession
Extra yield new supply must offer to clear the market.

↓Yields down · prices up

The holder gains; the buyer accepts less to lend.

rallied, rally
Buyers came in — yields fell, prices rose.
fell, lower in yield
The plain form. What fell is the yield, so the price rose.
richened
The bond got dearer: higher price, lower yield.
bid, well bid
Sustained buying. A duration bid is buying concentrated at the long end.
bull steepening
Yields fell across the curve, the front end most.
bull flattening
Yields fell across the curve, the long end most.
dovish, a dove
Leaning towards easier policy. A dovish surprise pulls yields down, the front end most — a bull steepening.
flight to quality
A bid for government paper driven by risk aversion elsewhere.

Steeper and flatter on their own carry no direction — they describe the gap between two tenors, not whether yields rose or fell; the bull/bear prefix supplies it. Widened and narrowed on this page refer to breakevens, not to bond prices.

Yield levels and movements

Change in basis points over each horizon. Shading is a fixed scale per column (see key below), so a colour means the same thing today as it will next month. Red = yields higher, blue = yields lower.

Curve spreads

Long tenor minus short tenor, in basis points. A positive change means the curve steepened over that horizon.

Cross-market differentials

Same tenor, one market minus another, in basis points. Positive means the first-named market yields more. Each differential is quoted only on days both markets traded and both curves were published, so the date shown can sit behind the fresher of the two markets — a spread against a session the other market has not seen yet is not a spread.

Real yields, breakevens and gold

US real yields are the Treasury's own TIPS par curve; breakeven inflation is the nominal par curve less the real par curve, same publisher and same day. Gold is the LBMA PM auction. Gold rows show percentage moves, everything else basis points.
